Wildlife trafficking remains a major driver of extinction risk. Drawing on seizure records from 2015–2021, [UNODC](https://digitallibrary.un.org/record/4047217/files/1405455-EN.pdf) found that 40% of mammal, bird, reptile and amphibian species recorded were classified as threatened or near-threatened. The trade is [commonly valued at $7–23 billion a year](https://news.mongabay.com/2016/12/signatories-to-hanoi-statement-urged-to-do-more-to-combat-illegal-wildlife-trade/), and [current trends](https://digitallibrary.un.org/record/4047217/files/1405455-EN.pdf) suggest the world will miss the [UN's target](https://metadata.un.org/sdg/15.7) on ending it by 2030. An often-cited contributor is the rise of e-commerce platforms  ([Barbosa et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.21211)), where sellers can cut out middlemen and reach buyers directly. The upside of this paradigm shift is that it also moved the formerly covert market out into the open, where the digital footprint left by public listings presents an unprecedented monitoring opportunity. However, finding relevant adverts among vast numbers of unrelated, heterogeneous listings precludes exclusively manual review, necessitating some degree of automation for sustained monitoring ([Di Minin et al., 2026](https://www.nature.com/articles/s44358-026-00192-8)).

The conventional approach of training specialised classifiers to map input adverts to predefined, policy-specific labels requires substantial annotation effort and often produces models that lack robustness to distribution shift and generalise poorly to new domains. In contrast, the comparatively recent advent of pre-trained LLMs (intuitive fits for text-heavy domains such as advert screening) poses the converse problem, offering broader transfer and zero-shot inference but at historically prohibitive inference costs at scale.

Past approaches highlight this trade-off. For example, [Stringham et al. (2021)](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0254007) demonstrated effective supervised filtering of bird adverts, but required substantial manual annotation (~103 hours) and emphasised context dependence. More recently, [Barbosa et al. (2024)](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.18898) leveraged large-scale zero-shot advert filtering using XLM-RoBERTa with natural-language candidate labels, but did not evaluate its classification accuracy against human annotations. Their subsequent [LTS study](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.21211) proposed a weakly supervised learning approach aimed at reducing manual annotation workloads by selectively using LLM-generated pseudo-labels to train cheaper classifiers via active learning, but similarly emphasised their narrow domain specificity as well as the prohibitive costs of scaling an alternative zero-shot LLM pipeline, forecasting that GPT-4 would have cost over $17,000 to label 800,000 ads (over $21.25 per 1,000 ads) at the time of writing. [Antala et al. (2026)](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/ICWSM/article/view/42629) improved ivory-related classification of social media posts through task-specific LLM fine-tuning. However, their reliance on task-specific labelled data and fine-tuning leaves the problem of adapting specialised classifiers to new monitoring policies unresolved. As such, doing so economically remains a challenge.

In recent months, a promising middle-ground has emerged in the form of purpose-built **decision models**: language models optimised for choosing among discrete alternatives and returning scores or probabilities rather than generating prose. These models all make the same economic proposition in different forms: classification-like semantic reasoning over broad knowledge bases at low marginal cost, often with explicit choice probabilities and no need for task-specific training. This raises a clear research question:

***Can off-the-shelf decision models affordably deliver competitive screening performance across various wildlife advert monitoring tasks through prompt changes alone?***

To address this question, this study evaluates out-of-the-box performance of three prominent decision models against chat-LLM and published supervised-classifier baselines on three expert-labelled monitoring datasets from the literature. [Results](#overall-results) show promising zero-shot screening performance from these models, with balanced accuracy averages exceeding those achieved by both the chat-LLM (at lower average request latency and API costs) and the selected published supervised baselines (on the two datasets for which corresponding results were available, conditional on differences in data, inputs and evaluation methods). Perhaps most strikingly, a 4-bit quantised, 19.4 GB `d3` model running locally on a 32 GB consumer laptop achieved approximately 96% balanced accuracy across all reviewed datasets with no retraining, threshold tuning, or in-context learning, raising the possibility of extending coverage of existing wildlife-trade monitoring pipelines with negligible manual overhead.
