This study evaluates three decision models against previously published LLM and supervised classifier benchmarks as well as a contemporary chat LLM on three wildlife advert monitoring datasets from the literature. The result that decision models matched or exceeded supervised baselines through zero-shot policy changes alone suggests that competitive screening across distinct monitoring policies need not always require task-specific training. The strongest evidence for this came from the locally hosted `d3` model, which achieved ~96% balanced accuracy on each dataset with no training, threshold calibration, or demonstrative examples in its policy prompts. This opens an expedient route to effective screening: rather than constructing a task-specific training pipeline—a notoriously laborious and time-consuming process requiring significant technical expertise—simply specify a policy in natural language and screen adverts directly. By removing this bottleneck, the approach could make additional species, product categories, or monitoring questions more feasible to investigate for a wider range of researchers. However, with neither decision models nor `GPT-6 Luna Chat` leading every comparison and results varying across experimental conditions, these findings establish the approach as a promising option warranting further investigation rather than a replacement for trained classifiers. Comparison with historical baselines remains conditional on [reported confounds](#limitations).

The speed and low marginal inference cost of the approach remove further barriers. At a hosted maximum of $0.09 per 1,000 adverts across the conditions tested here, the advent of decision models could lower the financial bar for effective zero-shot screening enough to bring exploratory monitoring within reach of smaller research and conservation teams. While `GPT-6 Luna Chat` was also inexpensive, `GPT-6 Luna Decisions` maintained a clear speed advantage across matched runs. Increasing the concurrency of hosted runs enabled the entire 16,407-sample Live Birds dataset to be processed in minutes, establishing a practical route to further runtime cuts where hardware permits. The exception to this was the `d3` model, which, due to local hardware constraints, was limited to sequential inference at higher per-sample latency, thereby trading speed for API cost elimination. Without measuring total local hardware and energy costs, however, the overall cost–speed trade-off remains unclear.

It is also important to note when interpreting these results that headline metrics such as balanced accuracy are alone insufficient to determine whether screening is workable in practice. In the fight against illegal wildlife trafficking, the immediate opportunity presented by automated screening pipelines is improved monitoring and prioritisation rather than automated legal adjudication. The latter still requires human judgement, so screening is only useful insofar as the subsequent manual review workload stays manageable. Composite metrics can conceal such details. Where relevant adverts are rare, for example, even a modest FPR can yield many more false positives than genuine leads. In the case of balanced accuracy, which captures the mission-critical TPR-FPR trade-off but is insensitive to prevalence, this could lead to swamping reviewers without lowering the headline score. Operational evaluations should therefore report metrics such as TPR, FPR, prevalence, review time and missed-advert counts so that users can find a balance fit for their use case.

One route to achieving such a balance not covered by this project is via threshold tuning ([Fawcett, 2006](https://www.math.ucdavis.edu/~saito/data/roc/fawcett-roc.pdf)). Whilst beyond the scope of this particular study, the provision of confidence scores alongside decision model predictions permits users to tune decision thresholds to whatever operating point best serves their use case, such as maximising detections within a fixed review capacity or minimising missed leads for high-priority species. The ROC curves presented here illustrate what such tuning could achieve. However, selecting a threshold for a particular use case requires a clean, labelled dataset held out from any data used for evaluation, reintroducing some of the annotation effort that zero-shot screening otherwise avoids.

Further possible developments include testing whether the screening advantages reported here produce useful outcomes under real monitoring conditions. For example, matched studies could compare direct screening with decision-model pseudo-labels used to train classifiers, active learning, or cascades that escalate uncertain cases to more expensive reviewers. A rigorous comparison would aim to measure annotation and setup effort, full operating costs, throughput, reviewer time and independently validated leads at natural prevalence, with repeated runs and recorded model/prompt versions. Prospective collaborations could then assess investigative usefulness and downstream conservation outcomes.
